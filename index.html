II Act II · 6 min · theoretical framework

Marshall’s Lens

Before answering the questions, the theory we use to read the map.

The thesis

The physical landscape (rivers, mountains, seas, climate, resources) constrains and enables what leaders can do.

Geography does not decide everything, but every decision is taken within its limits. Technology “bends” some rules without abolishing them (Introduction).

MARSHALL [S1]
Applied to Russia

“Vast”: a giant that is open to the west and frozen to the north.

Marshall opens with Putin wondering why God did not put mountains in Ukraine. The North European Plain is an invasion highway to Moscow; the Arctic coast freezes; every sea exit passes a strait someone else controls. Hence the reflex: attack as defence, and buy depth with territory.

MARSHALL · CH. 1 [S1]
  1. Survive the plainMuscovy, exposed on all sides, throws off the Mongols (1480).
  2. Reach natural barriersVolga, Urals, Caucasus, Carpathians: expand until terrain helps.
  3. Reach the seasBaltic 1703, Black Sea 1783, Pacific 1860: the hunt for warm water.
  4. Build a bufferWarsaw Pact 1945: put space between the plain and Moscow.
  5. Defend the bufferGeorgia 2008, Crimea 2014, Ukraine 2022: resist NATO’s advance.

The five steps are our synthesis of Marshall’s chapter, not his wording.

How we use himMarshall is a journalist, and his approach is often called geographical determinism. Acemoglu and Robinson (2012) argue that institutions explain outcomes better than geography; Kaplan (2012) and Zeihan (2014) defend geography’s weight. For Russia the risk is greater: “geography made us do it” is also the Kremlin’s own justification. We adopt the framework and test it. Our position: geography explains Russia’s fears; it does not dictate its choices.

Memory of invasion

Where Russians live · the map is lopsided

West of the Urals vs the empty east

The Ural, Siberian and Far Eastern federal districts cover 76.8% of Russia but held 25.2% of its people in the 2021 census; the five western districts hold 74.8% on 23.2% of the land. Bubbles show the 22 cities on the map at their 2021 census size.

VERIFIED FACT · ROSSTAT [S30, S32]
Latest data point · demography

Rosstat’s last published estimate is 146,119,928 people on 1 Jan 2025 [S26]. Russia recorded 1.222 m births in 2024, the fewest since 1999 [S34]. Since 2025 Rosstat no longer publishes monthly births and deaths [S18]; demographer Alexei Raksha estimates about 1.18 m births in 2025, an unofficial figure [S16]. Rosstat’s pessimistic scenario sees 130.6 m people by 2046 [S17].

Myth vs data

“Siberia is Russia’s future.” The Far Eastern district is 40.6% of Russia’s area but has 5.4% of its people, fewer than the city of Moscow alone [S32]. Its natural partner next door is China [S19].

Pacific · resources · emptiness

The Far East

Map unavailable offline.
  • Amur and Ussuri taken from China in 1858–60
  • 11 federal subjects, from Buryatia to Chukotka

Our analysisMoscow sits where the empire began: exposed, central, and therefore obsessed with control. The Far East is where the empire ends: Pacific-facing, resource-rich and dependent on a neighbour far larger in people. Marshall’s thesis at the regional scale.

Act IV · Part 2 · 0.5 min · critical counterpoint

But Geography Did Not Sit at the Table

Abu Dhabi produced no deal because of political choices in Moscow, Kyiv, Washington and Europe, not because of the terrain.

What holds up

Marshall’s 2015 buffer logic anticipated the pattern: after Crimea, the fight moved to the land corridor and Donbas. Russia now spends 7.5% of GDP and a record 20% of government spending on the military [S11]. Ports like Ust-Luga remain exposed [S7].

Where it needs qualification

The world’s largest nuclear arsenal [S27] makes a land invasion of Russia implausible, so “the open plain” is partly a narrative. The war pushed Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) into NATO [S31]: aggression produced the encirclement it feared. Institutions and regime type matter (Acemoglu and Robinson).

In the headline itself

Moscow widened the talks to Europe’s “security architecture” [S2]; Kyiv rejected giving up land it holds [S3]; Washington floated trade-offs [S5]. Geography set the stakes; politics sets the outcome.

The Future and the Answer

Geography that remains
  • The largest territory on Earth and strategic depth
  • Oil, gas, metals and grain from the black-earth belt
  • Arctic coast: Rosatom forecasts record Northern Sea Route cargo in 2026 [S13, S15]
  • Pipelines and a long border with China [S20, S21]
  • The largest nuclear arsenal [S27]
Challenges · mostly political and human
  • Shrinking population: fewest births since 1999 [S34, S17]
  • War economy: 7.5% of GDP on the military [S11]
  • Junior partner to China in trade and energy [S19, S21]
  • Thawing permafrost under about 65% of the country [S33]
  • Less transparent statistics and less free press [S18]

Scenarios to 2040 OUR ANALYSIS

Answer to the research question

History

An open plain made Muscovy insecure; expansion to rivers, mountains and seas was the answer, from Kazan to the Pacific. Each gain created new borders to defend, at the cost of the peoples absorbed.

People

A multi-ethnic empire before a nation, bound by a strong centre, Orthodoxy and the memory of invasion, and crowded into the west of a mostly empty land.

Power

Geography still gives Russia size, resources and the Arctic. Abu Dhabi shows Moscow still thinks in buffers. The limits it faces now are demographic, economic and political.

“Geography explains Russia’s fear. It does not decide Russia’s choices.”
